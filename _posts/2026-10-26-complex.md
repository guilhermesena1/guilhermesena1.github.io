# Introduction

The International Mathematics Competition for Univeristy Students (IMC) takes
place every year in Blagoevgrad, Bulgaria. The contest invites students from all
over the world to a two-day competition. Each day, you are presented 5 problems,
worth 10 points each, for a total of 100 points over both days.

Problems are roughly sorted in increasing level of difficulty, with problem 1
being the easiest, and 5 being the hardest. Problem 1 serves as a little
confidence boost, which you are basically expected to solve.

This is the first problem of day 2 from IMC 2013, a competition I had the chance
to attend.

I was awared 0 points for the day. I was stuck at this problem for 5 hours to no
avail.

# The problem

The
[statement](https://www.imc-math.org.uk/archive/problems/2013/IMC_2013_Day_2_Problems_and_Solutions.pdf)
is so simple you could mistake it for a problem you cross off in any high school
math book:

> Let $$z$$ be a complex number with $$\lvert z+1\rvert > 2$$. Prove that $$\lvert z^3+1\rvert >1$$.

That's it. That's the problem. I invite you to give it a try before reading
further.

# My solution

As I'm terrified of flying, in a recent trip I decided to pick up this problem
after 13 years and give it a serious shot. There are a few ways we can
parametrize a complex number:

1. Set $$z = a + bi$$, for $$a, b \in \mathbb{R}$$.
2. Set $$z = r(\cos \phi + i \sin  \phi )$$, for $$r \in \mathbb{R}, \phi \in
   [0, 2\pi)$$.
3. Set $$z = re^{i \phi}$$.

Typically, parametrization 3 is the best because exponential operations are
easier to manipulate than trigonometric ones.
In fact, since $$ |z + 1| > 2 $$, we can define $$ z + 1 = re^{i \phi} $$, with $$ r > 2 $$.

Now let us look at $$\lvert z^3 + 1 \rvert$$, which can be factored as $$\lvert z^3+1 \rvert =
\lvert z+1 \rvert \lvert z^2-z+1\rvert = r\lvert z^2-z+1 \rvert$$.

In theory, if we prove that $$\lvert z^2-z+1 \rvert \geq 1/2$$, we are done, but
this is not necessary for the proof to be complete. This assumption can be
violated but an increase in $\lvert z+1 \rvert$ can counter-balance this.
However, since this is problem 1, we can take a leap of faith and try to prove
that statement.

Substituting $$z=re^{i \phi} - 1$$ and using both $$\lvert w \rvert ^2 = w
\bar{w}$$ and $$w + \bar{w} = 2\cos\phi$$, we have:

$$ \lvert z^2 - z + 1 \rvert ^2 = \lvert (re^{i\phi} - 1)^2 - re^{i \phi} + 2
\rvert ^2 = \lvert r^2 e^{2 i \phi} - 3 r e^{i \phi} + 3 \rvert ^2 $$

$$ = (r^2 e^{2i \phi} - 3r e^{i \phi} + 3)(r^2 e^{-2i\phi} - 3re^{-i \phi} + 3)$$

$$ = r^4 - 3 r^3 e^{i \phi} + 3 r^2 e^{2i \phi} - 3 r^3 e^{-i \phi} + 9 r^2 - 9 r e^{i
\phi} + 3r^2 e^{-2i \phi} - 9re^{-i \phi} + 9$$

$$ = r^4 + 9r^2 + 9 - 6 r^3 \cos \phi -18r \cos \phi + 6r^2 \cos 2 \phi$$

Set aside the amount of math we had to do here without getting any terms
incorrectly, this expression is very had to take control of. First we recall
that $$r > 2$$, so we know the sign of many of these terms. Let's maybe fix a
value of $$\phi$$ and see how this entire expression varies with $$r$$. Define

$$ f(r) = r^4 + 9r^2 + 9 - 6 r^3 \cos \phi - 18r \cos \phi + 6 r^2 \cos 2
\phi.$$

The term $$r^4$$ seems to be leading in this equation. Moreover, it seems like
most of the high powers of $$r$$ are positive. What if took a **second** leap of
faith and tried to prove that $f$ is increasing in $r$? To prove that, we need
to prove that $$f'(r) > 0$$ for all $$\phi$$. Calculating $f'(r)$ we get:

$$
f'(r) = 4r^3 + 18 r - (18r^2 + 18)\cos \phi + 12r \cos 2\phi.
$$

The term $$\cos 2 \phi$$ is a little hectic to work with. Let's use $$\cos 2
\phi = 2\cos ^2 \phi - 1$$ and rearrange:

$$
f'(r) = 4r^3 +6r + [24r \cos ^2 \phi - 18(r^2 + 1) \cos \phi].
$$

To control for any value of $$\phi$$, let's complete the square:

$$
f'(r) = 4r^3 + 6 r + 6r \left[ 4 \cos ^2 \phi - 2 \frac{3}{4} \left(r +
\frac{1}{r} \right) 2 \cos \phi + \frac{9}{16}\left(r +
\frac{1}{r}\right)^2 \right] - \frac{9}{16}\left( r + \frac{1}{r} \right)^2
$$

$$
= 4r^3 + 6r -\frac{9}{16}\left(r^2 + \frac{1}{r^2} + 2 \right) + 6r \left( 2\cos \phi + r
+ \frac{1}{r}\right)^2,
$$

and since $$r > 2$$, it suffices to prove that

$$
4r^3 - \frac{9}{16}r^2 + 6 r >
\frac{9}{16}\left(\frac{1}{4}+2\right)=\frac{81}{64},
$$

which is easy, since

$$
4r^3 - \frac{9}{16}r^2 + 6r = r \left(4r^2 - 2 \frac{9}{64} 2r + 6 \right)
$$

$$
= r\left[\left(2r - \frac{9}{64}\right)^2 + 6 - \frac{81}{4096}\right] \geq 2
\times \frac{24495}{4096} > \frac{81}{64}.
$$

In other words, for any value of $$\phi$$, $$f(r)$$ is increasing in $$r$$, so
its minimum value over all possible values of $$(r, \phi)$$ must occur at
$$r=2$$.

Plugging $$f(2)$$, we get

$$f(2) = 48\cos^2 \phi - 84 \cos \phi + 37.$$

By now we are experts at completing squares, so:

$$
f(2) = 12 \left( 4 \cos ^2 \phi - 7 \cos \phi + \frac{49}{16} - \frac{49}{16}
\right) + 37
$$

$$
 = 12 \left(2 \cos \phi - \frac{7}{4}\right)^2 + 37 - 12 \times \frac{49}{16}
$$

$$
\geq 37 - \frac{147}{4} = \frac{148 - 147}{4} = \frac{1}{4},
$$

with the minimum occurring for $$(r, \phi) = (2, \cos^{-1} \left( \frac{7}{8}
\right) ) \approx (2, \pm 0.5053)$$. Indeed, the marginal in $$\phi$$ for various values of $$r$$ is
shown below. The minimum is approximated here.

![Graph of f(r) for various values of r, including the absolute minimum for
r=2](https://i.ibb.co/MDNrngzH/graph.jpg)

Working through this problem, I even forgot about the extreme turbulence I
endured, and with this solution I can finally get some sleep without the ghost
of a supposedly "easy" problem 1 haunting me.



# I do not like the official solution

The [official
solution](https://www.imc-math.org.uk/archive/problems/2013/IMC_2013_Day_2_Problems_and_Solutions.pdf)
got to the point above where we defined $$f(\phi)$$, then did the following:

$$ r^4 + 9r^2 + 9 - (6r^3 + 18r) \cos \phi + 6r^2 \cos 2 \phi$$

$$ = r^4 + 9r^2 + 9 - (6r^3 + 18r)\cos\phi+6r^2(2\cos^2 \phi - 1)$$

$$ = 12\left(r\cos\phi - \frac{r^2+3}{4}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{4}(r^2-3)^2 > 0
+\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}.$$

I find it particularly offensive that they dedicate one entire line to expanding
the very well-knon $$\cos 2 \phi$$ formula, then pull out a factorization from
the depths of hell that concludes the problem. Finding that the minimum occurs
at $$r=2$$ makes everything much easier, and it would be nice to add that proof
there too.

Were you able to solve the problem yourself? Is there an easier solution that
does not require crazy factorization skills? I'd love to know!
